The Learning Collider

Physics-first AI, AI-first physics.

After more than a decade of running, the Large Hadron Collider has revealed no physics beyond the Standard Model. The case that such physics exists is as strong as it ever was. So the interesting question is no longer whether we have looked hard enough. It is whether we have looked widely enough.

The limit is us

The binding constraint on discovery at the energy frontier is rarely stated plainly, because it is not flattering: it is not nature, and it is not the data. It is human effort. Every search is built by hand, by people, over years. Only a small fraction of the signatures the data could reveal has ever been examined — not because the rest were ruled out, but because nobody had the years to spare. The blind spots are not physical. They are sociological.

Systematic coverage

A search that is built once and configured many times can cover in months what would otherwise take person-centuries. That is an engineering problem, and engineering problems yield. We are building the engine: one validated pipeline, one background model, one statistical treatment — pointed, again and again, at spectra nobody has had the time to look at.

Honest at scale

Coverage without discipline is just a faster way to be wrong. Ask a thousand questions of the same dataset and something will always look significant. So every hypothesis we test is charged against a finite, explicitly budgeted discovery allowance: as the catalogue of searches grows, the threshold each one must clear rises with it, so that a thousandfold more searches produce no more false discoveries than a single one. Honesty at scale is the design constraint, not a caveat at the end.

A map of what has been looked at

The output of a systematic search is not only a limit or a bump. It is a statement — for the first time — of which possibilities have been probed, to what sensitivity, and which remain blind. If nature has put something within reach, this is how it gets found. If it has not, the field gains a quantified account of its own reach, and keeps the instrument.

Machines build. People decide.

AI agents can write, test and harden the engine, and they are very good at it. They do not make scientific decisions. Production runs deterministically; every published number is a transparent, classical calculation a referee can follow line by line. We let the methods be as modern as they need to be and the inference as old-fashioned as it has to be. That is what physics-first AI means here.

Why now

Run 3 is complete. The accelerator is in a long shutdown. The High-Luminosity LHC will deliver most of the data the LHC will ever produce — and the instrument that decides how that data gets searched has to be built and validated on the data we already have.

Where the name comes from

RODEM — Robust Deep Density Models for High-Energy Particle Physics and Solar Flare Analysis — was an SNSF Sinergia project that ran from 2020 to 2024, shared with colleagues in computer science and in astronomy. It is why this group exists in its current form. It paid for the first generation of students and postdocs here, who went on to build CURTAINs, the ν-flows family and the first particle-cloud diffusion models; it is the reason a particle physics group in Geneva started publishing at NeurIPS; and it set the habit of treating a method as a deliverable in its own right, released and documented, rather than as scaffolding for one measurement.

The project ended in 2024. The name did not. The datasets are still the RODEM Jet Datasets, the code still lives at github.com/rodem-hep, and the group is still called RODEM — which is either sentimental or good branding, depending on the day.
